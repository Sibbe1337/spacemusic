## Draft 1: Selling an Independent Label Catalog: What to Prepare

### Start with the rights your label can actually offer

A label name, distributor dashboard and release list are useful starting points. They are not, by themselves, a complete explanation of the interest you want to sell. Before seeking an offer, put each proposed recording next to the agreement and income records that relate to it.

SPACE considers revenue-generating catalogs across genres through an individual review. The initial application is free and does not commit you to a sale. An offer is not guaranteed. See the [published offer policy](https://spacemusic.io/pricing.md).

### Build a release-level review pack

For each recording, list title, artist, version and ISRC when available. Identify the party offering the rights, the agreement supporting its position, the territory and term, and any other party who may need to approve a transaction. Put unknowns in an explicit follow-up column rather than treating them as cleared.

Keep recording rights separate from the underlying compositions. They are distinct copyright works, as the [U.S. Copyright Office explains](https://www.copyright.gov/register/pa-sr.html). Local law and the actual contracts need individual professional review.

Match royalty statements to the release list, identifying the periods and sources included. Provide the relevant artist/distribution agreements and explanations of deductions or continuing payment obligations. This is a suggested preparation pack, not an automatic approval checklist.

### Worked example: a mixed-rights label

Hypothetical only: a label has 24 releases. Its team identifies 16 as supported by ownership agreements, five as term-limited licenses and three with missing documents. It prepares separate rows for those groups and flags the missing evidence. It does not represent all 24 as unconditionally transferable.

The reviewer can then ask focused questions about the proposed scope and documents. This example is not a SPACE transaction, qualification decision or valuation.

### Limits and next step

Contract assignment provisions, third-party obligations and actual ownership cannot be resolved by a generic web page. Keep the proposed sale of catalog assets distinct from a sale of the label company; this draft does not claim that SPACE provides company-acquisition services.

Use the [seller document checklist](https://spacemusic.io/blog/music-catalog-sale-documents-checklist/) to organize the first pack. Then [request a free catalog review](https://spacemusic.io/apply/). Do not send private contracts in public comments or analytics fields. Have an independent adviser review any proposed agreement.

## Draft 2: Can You Sell a Hip-Hop or R&B Catalog With Collaborators?

### Your credits and your sale scope need to tell the same story

An artist may have released a track with a producer, featured performer, beat license or third-party sample. Before proposing a catalog sale, make a track-by-track list of the recordings and the interest you intend to offer. This is about making the review understandable, not assuming that every collaboration prevents a sale.

The recording and the song underneath it are distinct copyright works. Do not assume that owning one means owning the other. [U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/engage/musicians/). Ask an independent adviser to interpret your contracts and the applicable law.

### Prepare the collaborators-and-documents matrix

Start with the release identifiers and credited contributors. For each track, identify the relevant producer agreement, feature agreement, beat license and any clearance documentation you have. Note missing or disputed material explicitly. Provide royalty statements for the relevant recordings and periods when requested.

Where an agreement is unclear about a proposed transaction, write down the question for your adviser rather than describing the recording as unrestricted. This is a document-preparation workflow, not a legal finding about the license.

### Worked example: selected tracks, not your entire career

Hypothetical only: an artist proposes 12 existing recordings and excludes everything released after an agreed cutoff. Eight recordings have a complete document pack, two have a producer-agreement question and two are missing clearance records. The artist marks these distinctions in the schedule and requests an initial review of the proposed scope.

No price or acceptance is implied. Missing documents may require follow-up or a different proposed scope. The example is not based on a customer catalog.

### Limits and next step

SPACE considers all genres, including hip-hop and R&B, but reviews income and transferable rights individually. The application does not provide sample clearance, settle a dispute, or establish ownership. Its [offer policy](https://spacemusic.io/pricing.md) explains the free initial review and conditional process.

Read the existing [music-rights guide](https://spacemusic.io/guides/music-rights-101/) and [document checklist](https://spacemusic.io/blog/music-catalog-sale-documents-checklist/), then [request a free catalog review](https://spacemusic.io/apply/). Only submit material you are entitled to share.

## Draft 3: Selling a Pop or Rock Back Catalog Without Confusing Your Next Release

### Define the recordings, versions and people involved

An existing album catalog can include original masters, remasters, live recordings and alternate versions. A useful sale proposal identifies the specific recordings and interests being considered, rather than using the artist or band name as a substitute for a schedule.

Prepare a release list, relevant agreements, supporting royalty records and a list of any co-owners or parties whose approval needs checking. Keep unanswered questions visible. Do not assume a distribution account proves sole ownership.

### Separate the catalog from future work

Discuss the proposed release cutoff and exclusions before accepting terms. Ask what the agreement says about later recordings, approvals and ongoing responsibilities. This draft does not promise that every contract leaves every future right untouched.

The distinction between recording rights and compositions also matters here. The [U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/register/pa-sr.html) treats them as separate works; the applicable law and individual agreements determine the actual scope available for a sale.

### Worked example: two albums and an excluded live release

Hypothetical only: a band proposes the original studio recordings on two released albums. Its draft schedule excludes a live album, alternate mixes and its unreleased third album. It lists release/version identifiers and asks an adviser to confirm who can authorize the transaction and which approvals are needed.

The band then provides the revenue records associated with the proposed recordings, rather than treating all income in its distributor account as revenue from the sale scope. This illustrates preparation, not an offer or a completed SPACE deal.

### Limits and next step

There is no genre-based price promise. SPACE's initial review is free, and any offer depends on the catalog, income evidence and rights. Payment is subject to the completed agreement and satisfied transfer conditions. [Offer policy](https://spacemusic.io/pricing.md).

Use the [partial-versus-full sale comparison](https://spacemusic.io/compare/partial-sale-vs-full-sale/) and the [offer checklist](https://spacemusic.io/blog/music-catalog-offer-checklist/) to prepare questions. Then [request a free catalog review](https://spacemusic.io/apply/). Obtain independent legal advice before agreeing to transfer rights.
