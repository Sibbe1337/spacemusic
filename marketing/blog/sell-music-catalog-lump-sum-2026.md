# How to Sell Your Music Catalog for a Lump Sum in 2026

Selling your music catalog for a lump sum means exchanging specified music rights or royalty income for an upfront payment. Start by defining what you own, documenting its earnings and requesting an offer. Before signing, compare the cash you would actually receive with the rights and future income you would give up.

You do not necessarily have to sell every track or every right. A sale can cover selected recordings, a percentage interest or a defined income stream. This guide focuses on evaluating that trade-off, rather than promising a particular price for your catalog.

SPACE is a direct catalog buyer and publishes this guide for independent artists, producers, labels and catalog owners. The information is general, not personalized legal, tax or financial advice. Have independent advisers review your specific transaction.

## What are you actually selling?

Before discussing a price, separate the **master recording** from the **underlying composition**. They are distinct copyrights; owning a recording does not itself mean owning the song's publishing rights. The [U.S. Copyright Office explains the difference](https://www.copyright.gov/register/pa-sr.html).

A useful sale description identifies:

- **The assets:** named recordings or compositions, with identifiers where available.
- **Your interest:** the ownership or income share you control, not your collaborators' shares.
- **The income:** for example, recording royalties rather than publishing income you intend to retain.
- **The scope:** territories, duration, licensing authority and any rights excluded from the transfer.

Do not assume that a payment called a "buyout" covers every right forever. Transfers can be limited by time or territory, and a royalty-income transaction may differ from selling the underlying copyright. [U.S. Copyright Office guidance on transfers](https://www.copyright.gov/recordation/).

For a deeper rights comparison, read [selling masters versus selling publishing](/compare/selling-masters-vs-selling-publishing/).

## How do you calculate a lump-sum offer?

A revenue multiple is a way to express a price relative to annual earnings. The calculation only helps if the income and the offer cover the same rights, share and period.

**Illustrative price = annual earnings attributable to the interest being sold x assumed multiple.**

Suppose royalty statements show an average of $1,400 per month attributable to the rights and share you propose to sell, after applicable distributor deductions and collaborator allocations. That is $16,800 over 12 months.

| Illustration | Calculation | Illustrative amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual earnings | $1,400 x 12 months | $16,800 |
| Assumed 4x multiple | $16,800 x 4 | $67,200 |
| Assumed 6x multiple | $16,800 x 6 | $100,800 |

**These are arithmetic examples, not market benchmarks or offers.** The 4x and 6x assumptions were chosen to explain the calculation, not to state what buyers pay in 2026. A calculator cannot establish the price someone will offer for your catalog.

Check the denominator carefully. If a statement reports the whole catalog's earnings but you own only half, using the full figure would overstate the income you can offer. Likewise, selling half of your existing interest is different from selling your entire interest. Real partial-sale prices need not scale proportionally.

SPACE reviews documented royalty and label revenue, its consistency, the ownership interest and supporting records. Concentration in a few tracks and the mix of income sources also matter. Reference ranges are indicative, not guaranteed offers. [SPACE's offer policy](/pricing.md).

For the broader methodology, see our [music catalog valuation guide](/guides/music-catalog-valuation-2026/).

## Compare net proceeds, not just the headline price

An offer of $67,200 is not necessarily $67,200 available to spend. Ask for a closing statement that separates the purchase price, amounts withheld, existing balances to settle, transaction costs and payment timing.

For example, if a hypothetical $67,200 transaction required $3,000 in combined deductions and adviser costs, the amount remaining would be **$64,200 before tax**. Those deductions are illustrative, not SPACE fees or an estimate of your tax bill.

Have your advisers help you compare:

1. **Cash at closing:** the amount payable immediately, versus holdbacks, installments or conditional payments.
2. **Income retained:** royalties and rights you keep after the sale.
3. **Income surrendered:** what happens under declining, stable and growing earnings scenarios.
4. **Remaining commitments:** any warranties, claims exposure, cooperation requirements or delivery obligations.
5. **Your purpose:** whether the proceeds meet a defined need without creating an unaffordable income gap.

The calendar alone does not make a sale attractive. Evaluate an actual offer against your circumstances rather than assuming that every catalog owner should sell in 2026.

## Can you stop relying on monthly streaming royalties?

A catalog sale can exchange a defined future income stream for cash at closing, but it does not create a permanent replacement salary. You stop receiving the income you sell for the agreed period. Decide how the proceeds and any retained earnings would cover your needs before treating a lump sum as financial security.

### Check income changes separately from reporting delays

There is no fixed Spotify payment for every stream. Spotify describes paying rights holders according to streamshare, with artists paid under their respective agreements. A published average is not a rate your recordings are guaranteed to earn. [Spotify's explanation of royalty payments](https://loudandclear.byspotify.com/faqs/how-do-artists-and-songwriters-get-paid/).

Likewise, Apple's published per-play illustration describes a particular period and subscription context, not a guaranteed 2026 payment to an individual artist. Avoid valuing your catalog from an undated list of Spotify, Apple Music and TIDAL rates. [Apple Music's royalty explanation](https://artists.apple.com/support/1124-apple-music-insights-royalty-rate).

A changing bank deposit does not necessarily mean that listening demand changed by the same amount. DistroKid, for example, says service reports can cover activity from earlier months and arrive on different schedules. Reconcile the earning period, reporting date and withdrawal date before diagnosing a decline. [DistroKid's payment guidance](https://support.distrokid.com/hc/en-us/articles/360013547274-How-and-When-Do-I-Get-Paid).

Genre is not a substitute for this evidence. An ambient or lo-fi catalog may have repeat listeners, but that alone does not prove stable income. Check your own history and concentration in tracks and income sources instead of assuming that functional music cannot lose revenue.

### Plan the income gap after closing

First separate a temporary mismatch in payment dates from an ongoing shortfall. A cash-flow budget records when income arrives and expenses fall due, not just their monthly totals. The [CFPB's budgeting guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/budgeting-how-to-create-a-budget-and-stick-with-it/) explains this distinction.

For a sale scenario, subtract retained royalties and other income from the spending you need to cover. Then compare that remaining monthly gap with the proceeds actually available after costs, balances to settle and a separately assessed tax reserve.

**Hypothetical budget, not an offer or tax estimate:** assume $24,000 is available solely to cover that gap. With no investment return, new expenses or changes in income:

| Monthly gap after other income | Calculation | Months covered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $750 | $24,000 / $750 | 32 months |
| $1,000 | $24,000 / $1,000 | 24 months |
| $1,500 | $24,000 / $1,500 | 16 months |

This is a depletion calculation, not proof that selling beats keeping the catalog. Spending part of the same money on a studio project reduces what remains for recurring expenses; changing costs or income also changes the result. Do not count future royalties from the sold interest as income you still receive.

If the problem is a reporting delay rather than a long-term funding need, examine the timing before giving up years of income. Keeping the catalog, considering a partial transaction or reviewing an advance are alternatives to compare, not automatic recommendations. No funding route removes every financial or contractual risk.

## Direct sale, marketplace or royalty advance?

These are different ways to obtain capital, not interchangeable products. Compare the actual agreement, not just the label.

| Route | Basic structure | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct catalog sale | A buyer negotiates a purchase of defined rights or income with you. | What transfers, what remains yours, and what conditions must be met before payment? |
| Marketplace or auction | A platform connects a defined royalty asset with potential buyers. | What are the fees, minimum price, disclosure requirements and closing conditions? |
| Royalty advance | Upfront funding is exchanged for an agreed portion of future income under the contract. | What is the term, recoupment mechanism, retained income and treatment of future releases? |

### A direct buyer still reviews the catalog

SPACE reviews catalogs directly rather than requiring a public auction. Its application and initial review are free, with no listing fee or auction commission. Submitting a catalog does not guarantee an offer or oblige you to accept one. [SPACE's seller policy](/pricing.md).

### A marketplace does not guarantee a higher or lower price

Royalty Exchange's auction process lets sellers define the royalty asset and minimum starting price. If that price is not met, there is no sale. Its listing agreement also specifies the platform fee. Competition can affect the outcome, but it does not establish in advance which route will deliver better net proceeds for you. [Royalty Exchange's auction process](https://royaltyexchange.com/how-auctions-work-sellers).

### An advance is not automatically an interest-bearing loan

For example, **beatBread explicitly says its advances are not loans**: it describes them as purchases of a limited portion of revenues for a limited time. Its FAQ says it charges no interest, although fees apply. If the advance has not recouped by the end of the term, collection can continue until recoupment. [beatBread's FAQ](https://www.beatbread.com/faq).

That does not make an advance cost-free, nor does it mean all providers use the same structure. Check how much income you retain, which recordings are covered and when the revenue-sharing arrangement ends.

## Six steps from royalty statements to payment

### 1. Define the sale before estimating its value

List the tracks, rights and percentage you want reviewed. Mark anything you want excluded. Use a [catalog calculator](/calculator/) only as an indicative starting point, not as a binding offer or evidence of market value.

### 2. Organize the revenue evidence

Prepare monthly statements by payer, period and rights type. Keep recording income separate from publishing income, identify adjustments and avoid counting the same payment twice. Include the history requested by the buyer; requirements vary. Royalty Exchange, for example, asks for at least three years of statements for its auction process. [Its seller requirements](https://royaltyexchange.com/how-auctions-work-sellers).

For help distinguishing PRO income from recording royalties, read our [blanket-license and performance-royalty guide](/blog/what-is-a-blanket-license/).

### 3. Check ownership and existing commitments

Gather collaborator splits, label or distribution agreements, publishing administration terms, sample permissions and relevant licenses. Flag outstanding advances, disputes, security interests or restrictions rather than assuming they disappear when you sell.

Ask an adviser to confirm whether the proposed transaction needs third-party consent and whether you can transfer the exact interest being offered.

### 4. Request a review

Submit the catalog information and supporting documents through the buyer's application process. Ask what is still needed for a complete review and whether the next response will be an indicative estimate or a conditional purchase offer.

At SPACE, an initial offer typically takes **5-7 business days after the relevant catalog information is received**, subject to review. That is not a guaranteed application-to-payment deadline. [SPACE's timing policy](/pricing.md).

### 5. Review the agreement independently

Check that the contract's asset schedule matches the catalog you discussed. Review the purchase price, deductions, transferred share, territories, term, payment conditions, future-release clauses and any continuing obligations.

In the United States, a copyright ownership transfer generally requires a written instrument signed by the owner or authorized agent, except transfers by operation of law. Rules and formalities depend on the applicable jurisdiction. [17 U.S.C. section 204](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html#204).

### 6. Complete the agreed transfer and payment steps

Confirm who handles delivery, royalty payment directions, distributor changes and any necessary registration updates. Keep copies of the signed agreement, asset schedule and settlement records. Do not assume that an entire PRO account or every distribution arrangement automatically moves to the buyer.

SPACE pays by bank transfer after the agreement is complete and the rights-transfer requirements are satisfied. [Payment policy](/pricing.md).

## Can you keep some tracks and your future music?

There are two separate partial-sale questions: **which tracks are included**, and **what share of their rights or income changes hands**. Selling selected older recordings is not the same as selling a percentage interest across the whole catalog. Discuss both options rather than using "partial sale" without defining it.

Future music is outside a transaction expressly limited to specified existing works. But check the agreement for delivery commitments, options or clauses involving future releases before assuming they are excluded. Also distinguish artist attribution from ownership: ask for any agreed credit protections to be written into the contract.

See our [partial sale versus full sale comparison](/compare/partial-sale-vs-full-sale/) for the trade-offs.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a minimum catalog size or income?

There is no single industry-wide threshold established by this guide. Buyers and funding providers set their own requirements. Ask the buyer about your catalog rather than assuming that a particular track count, genre or annual income guarantees eligibility.

SPACE's cited [offer policy](/pricing.md) does not state a fixed minimum earnings requirement. Request an individual review rather than treating $2,000 per year as an established SPACE threshold.

### Will I receive all future royalties after a sale?

Not for the income interest you have sold, during the period covered by that transaction. You may retain income from excluded tracks, publishing rights or an unsold share. The agreement should identify both the transferred and retained interests.

### Is every catalog sale permanent?

No. Ownership transfers and income transactions can have different durations and scopes. Do not rely on the word "sale" alone; read the term and any applicable reversion or termination provisions with your adviser. [U.S. Copyright Office transfer guidance](https://www.copyright.gov/recordation/).

### What is a fair revenue multiple in 2026?

This guide does not establish a universal market range. Compare offers using the same earnings basis, rights, ownership share, term and net payment. An apparently higher multiple can still cover more rights or have less favorable conditions.

### What should I compare besides the offer price?

Compare cash at closing, fees and deductions, retained income, payment conditions, control over licensing and any continuing obligations. Independent legal and tax review can help you understand the consequences before you commit.

### Does selling a catalog automatically transfer my artist profiles?

Do not assume that the rights transaction also settles access to artist profiles, distributor accounts, social channels or future releases. Identify those separately in the agreement and confirm the operational steps with the relevant distributor or platform. Ask who controls catalog delivery and metadata, what access you retain and whether a transfer could affect existing releases before making changes.
