# Music Catalog Sale Documents: A Seller's Checklist

A music catalog sale starts with evidence of what you own and what that ownership earns. Prepare royalty statements, a track-and-rights schedule, relevant agreements and a short list of unresolved issues. Keep the original files: a summary spreadsheet helps a reviewer navigate the evidence, but does not replace it.

This checklist is for independent artists, producers, labels and catalog owners preparing for an initial review. The exact documents depend on the rights, countries and transaction. It is not a legal opinion, a promise of eligibility or a requirement to upload everything in the first application. SPACE is a prospective direct buyer, not your independent adviser.

## Start with the rights you propose to sell

Write a one-paragraph scope before collecting files. Identify the recordings or compositions, your owned share, the share offered, the territories and any proposed time limit. Be explicit about exclusions: future releases, particular tracks, retained publishing or existing licenses.

Recording and composition rights are distinct. The [US Copyright Office's Circular 56A](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ56a.pdf) explains that a recording and its underlying musical composition are separate works. Owning one does not establish ownership of the other. For background, see [master rights](/glossary/master-rights/) and [publishing rights](/glossary/publishing-rights/).

If you own half of the recording rights and want to sell half of that interest, say so. Do not describe this as selling half of the entire recording. The same precision is needed when matching income to the rights offered.

## Gather the royalty statements behind your income

Use original exports from distributors, publishers, collection organizations and direct licensees for the income included in the sale. Prepare the available recent history and keep earlier records that explain the trend. The reviewer should confirm the periods needed; this checklist does not introduce a universal minimum history or income threshold.

For each source, identify:

- The reporting period and whether it refers to usage, accounting or payment dates.
- The currency and any conversion used in your summary.
- The recording or work identifier and territory, where supplied.
- Gross amounts, distributor or administration deductions, third-party payouts and net receipts.
- Adjustments, reserves, refunds, recoupment and unpaid balances.
- The matching payout record, where available, without exposing unrelated account details.

Reconcile totals instead of adding the same income twice. A distributor's statement, a payout confirmation and a bank deposit may describe the same money. A royalty statement may also show activity earned in one month but paid later. Keep those distinctions in the summary.

Streaming analytics can explain a spike or a change in platform mix, but they are not royalty statements. [Spotify explains that payments go to rights holders](https://loudandclear.byspotify.com/faqs/how-do-artists-and-songwriters-get-paid/); the contracts in that chain affect what artists and songwriters receive. Do not turn a listener count into documented income using a generic per-stream rate.

## Build a track and rights schedule

One row per recording or work makes it easier to match the proposed assets to their contracts and earnings. Keep recording ownership, composition ownership and an entitlement to receive royalties separate rather than putting all three in one ambiguous percentage column.

| Field | What to record | What to flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | Track title, version, artist, release and original release date | Alternate titles, remasters or duplicate rows |
| Recording identifier | Existing ISRC and distributor identifiers | Missing or conflicting identifiers |
| Composition identifier | ISWC where assigned, work title and writers | Unmatched works or conflicting registrations |
| Rights interest | Right type, territory, owned share and proposed sold share | Administration rights mistaken for ownership |
| Income mapping | Statement source, period and matching identifier | Earnings not attributable to the proposed assets |
| Restrictions | Agreement reference, term, consent and recoupment notes | Open questions that require review |

An identifier is a matching tool, not proof of title. [IFPI's ISRC guidance](https://isrc.ifpi.org/why-use-isrc/ownership) says that recording ownership can change without changing the ISRC. [ISWC guidance](https://www.iswc.org/iswc) explains that the musical-work identifier does not specify copyright ownership shares. Attach the agreements that support those separate ownership claims. Our [metadata overview](/glossary/metadata/) explains the basic vocabulary.

## Collect agreements that affect the sale

Gather the executed versions and relevant amendments, not just unsigned templates or an email describing a deal. A reviewer needs to understand both how you acquired the interest and which obligations still attach to it.

- Co-writer and collaborator agreements, split sheets and any subsequent changes.
- Producer, featured-artist, performer and label agreements affecting the recordings or royalty participation.
- Distribution, publishing and administration agreements, including assignment and termination provisions.
- Sample, interpolation, beat and other third-party permissions relevant to the proposed assets.
- Sync, app, library and other licenses that may continue after a sale.
- Earlier acquisition or assignment documents, financing agreements and recoupment statements.
- Evidence of authority to act for a company, estate or another owner where applicable, shared through the reviewer's private process.

For US copyright ownership transfers, [17 USC Section 204](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html#204) generally requires a signed writing, with the statutory exception for transfers by operation of law. Other jurisdictions and agreements need their own analysis. A split sheet or distributor account access should not be assumed to settle every question about transferability. Have a qualified independent lawyer review uncertain rights or permissions.

## Make an unresolved-issues list

Missing information is more useful when it is identified clearly than when it is filled with guesses. Use an issue, affected asset, supporting file and next-step format. Keep collaborators' private contact details in the private review process, not a public spreadsheet.

Examples of issues to disclose include a disputed ownership percentage, an unsigned amendment, a license whose exclusivity is unclear, a sample clearance that cannot be located, an unrecouped advance, or a distributor that must approve an assignment. These are examples of review questions, not claims about your catalog or a reason to conceal a problem.

For each item, state whether it is unresolved, awaiting a document or already reviewed by your adviser. Do not relabel an unresolved issue as cleared merely to complete the checklist. It may need resolution before a commercial offer or closing can proceed.

## Share the package privately and keep an index

Use folders for income, rights, agreements and metadata, with an index explaining what each file contains. Include a prepared date and version so a reviewer does not mistake an older statement or draft for the current one. Preserve originals and label summaries separately.

Before sending documents, confirm the recipient and sharing channel. Do not publish royalty statements, bank details, collaborator contact lists or contracts on an open website. Do not send account passwords, login tokens or one-time codes. Ask which redactions are acceptable; removing essential rights, dates or financial context can make evidence impossible to verify.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need every document before I apply?

Not necessarily. Start with an accurate description of what you control and what evidence is available. Identify missing documents honestly. The reviewer can confirm what is needed next; do not assume an incomplete file has already been verified.

### Is a spreadsheet enough to prove revenue?

A spreadsheet is a useful reconciliation, not independent proof of its own inputs. Retain the original royalty statements and matching payment records so a reviewer can trace the figures and identify deductions or reporting delays.

### Does an ISRC prove that I own a recording?

No. An ISRC identifies a recording. Ownership and transferability need supporting agreements and other relevant records; the identifier does not replace them.

### Can I remove private information before sharing files?

Ask the reviewer which redactions are acceptable and use an agreed private channel. Remove irrelevant sensitive information, but do not silently remove material terms or evidence needed to assess the proposed rights and income.

### Does preparing these documents guarantee a higher price?

No. A clear package can make a review easier, but price and eligibility depend on the catalog, rights, risks and agreed terms. No price uplift or purchase outcome is promised.
